# Chapter One: The Lead

The clinic had spent money on armored smartglass and nowhere else.

On the way in, Mari had watched rain crawl down the dark panes while the noodle stall across the avenue smeared a broken red holo-menu over their surface. Inside, old intake kiosks tracked the waiting room through cracked camera lenses. The vinyl chairs had been repaired with strips of medical sealant, and somebody had clipped a moisture sensor to a dead ficus; its green status light continued to insist the plant was healthy. A dispenser beside reception accepted credchips for cups of synthetic coffee. The air smelled of disinfectant, overheated circuitry, and burnt synth-caf.

Mari had been staring at the same unanswered question on Eli’s intake screen for three minutes.

Across from her, Eli leaned forward and took his screen back.

Eli: “You’ve been reading that question so long it’s starting to feel invasive.”

Mari: “You skipped it.”

Eli: “I was building suspense. Also, it’s my intake.”

Mari: “You leave things blank.”

Eli: “Because ‘none of your business’ is never one of the options.”

Mari: “You’re my little brother. Your business has always been my business.”

Eli: “By six minutes. Six minutes is not a medical qualification.”

Eli gave her a thin, satisfied smile and scrolled through the form. His left thumb moved carefully over the screen, as if each word had to be persuaded to stay still. Mari tried not to watch it. She had become very good at not watching things.

Eli passed the screen back.

Eli: “They want to know whether I’ve had any new symptoms.”

Mari: “They always want to know that.”

Eli fixed her with a dead-serious stare.

Eli: “Maybe this time I’ll surprise them.”

Mari looked back at him, slightly annoyed despite herself.

Eli let the solemn expression break and raised both hands in surrender.

Eli: “No new symptoms. Just a joke. Don’t call emergency services.”

Mari: “I am emergency services.”

Eli: “Neighborhood mobile care. That’s a different brand of disappointment. Besides, you’re not supposed to arrest your brother for making bad jokes.”

Mari took the screen and tapped through the boxes. Tingling. Intermittent loss of grip. Fatigue. The words were all too small for what they meant. She hated that the clinic made them select symptoms like ordering a meal.

A sharp electronic chirp sounded near the vending machines.

A woman had stopped beside the self-check station with one hand on the counter. She was small-boned, pale beneath the cyan wash of the display, with dark hair cut bluntly across her forehead. Fine seams caught the light at her temples and along the curve of one cheek. More hardware disappeared beneath the collar of her jacket, where a narrow line of biometal followed the side of her neck.

She hadn’t noticed the sensor lead caught around her wrist.

The lead ran from a disposable patch on the inside of her arm to the check station. As she shifted her bag onto her shoulder, it drew tight. The little port at the counter gave another warning chirp.

Mari: “Miss?”

The woman kept walking.

Mari stood before she thought about it.

Mari: “Hey—your lead.”

The woman turned. Her eyes went first to Mari’s face, then to the cable pulling at her wrist. For one second, nothing changed in her expression.

Then she looked annoyed.

Sanya: “Of course it is.”

The bag strap had caught the lead and pulled it into a tight loop. Mari reached for it, then stopped with her hand halfway up.

Mari: “Can I?”

The woman looked at Mari’s hand. A small pause. Not suspicion exactly. Calculation.

Sanya: “Please. Before it decides I’m stealing clinic property.”

Mari eased the cable free. The woman stood very still while Mari worked. Up close, the hardware at her neck was not decorative chrome. It was too precise for that, too integrated with the soft skin beneath it. The patch on her arm blinked a calm green.

Mari: “There.”

Sanya: “Heroic.”

Mari: “You were about to take half the station with you.”

Sanya: “Then it would have had a better morning than I did.”

Mari laughed before she could stop herself.

The woman looked at Mari again. Properly this time. Her expression did not soften, exactly, but something in it shifted—as if Mari had answered a question she had not heard asked.

Eli: “Velez.”

Velez’s gaze went past Mari. Whatever had nearly appeared on her face disappeared again.

Sanya: “Novak.”

Mari turned. Eli had risen from his chair, one hand braced on its arm. He looked between them with the wary interest of someone arriving halfway through a conversation and immediately deciding it was more interesting than his own.

Mari: “You two know each other?”

Eli: “Clinic celebrity.”

Velez snorted.

Sanya: “That implies people are pleased to see you.”

Eli: “We applied to the same academy. Years ago.”

Sanya: “Don’t make it sound romantic.”

Eli: “I got a scholarship. She got standards.”

For a moment Mari thought Sanya might smile. Instead, she looked at Eli’s cane leaning against the chair, then at the intake screen in Mari’s hand.

Sanya: “Congratulations.”

The word was clean enough to cut.

Nurse: “Mr. Novak?”

Eli reached for his cane. Mari moved automatically, but he was already standing.

Eli: “I’ll be twenty minutes. Try not to make any irreversible life decisions without me, big sister.”

Mari: “I make all my irreversible decisions with you.”

Eli: “Tragic.”

He followed the nurse down the corridor.

Mari watched until Eli turned the corner. When she looked back, Velez was still there, one hand resting lightly over the blinking patch on her arm.

Mari: “You don’t have to apologize for him.”

Sanya: “I wasn’t planning to.”

Mari: “Good.”

The corner of Velez’s mouth tilted.

Sanya: “You apologize for him a lot?”

Mari: “Only when he earns it.”

Sanya: “And does he?”

Mari glanced down the corridor.

Mari: “More often than he thinks.”

The patch chirped again. Velez looked at it with naked irritation, then tucked her sleeve over it.

Sanya: “Mari.”

Velez said the name as if testing it against something private.

Mari had not introduced herself. Then she remembered the visitor tag clipped to her own jacket. On Velez’s sensor patch, the patient display read SANYA VELEZ.

Mari: “Sanya.”

This time Sanya’s smile made it all the way through.

Sanya glanced toward the vending machine, where a can of something fluorescent had stalled halfway down its spiral.

Sanya: “That thing has been trying to sell the same can of piss-yellow energy drink since last winter.”

Mari followed her gaze.

Mari: “Maybe nobody wants piss-yellow energy drink.”

Sanya: “Nobody wants most things in this building. They want a test result that says they’re allowed to go home.”

The answer was too quick to be casual. Mari looked at her, but Sanya was still watching the trapped can.

Mari: “Do they ever say that?”

Sanya: “That you’re allowed to go home?”

Mari nodded.

Sanya considered it with more seriousness than the question deserved.

Sanya: “They say things like stable and manageable. They say we’ll keep an eye on it, which is comforting if you enjoy being watched by people paid to notice when you get worse.”

Mari felt the familiar reflex rise in her—the one that wanted to soften the room, find the useful thing to say, make the stranger regret less of what she had just admitted. It was the same reflex that made her carry spare pain patches in every jacket she owned, even though Eli insisted he could buy his own.

Mari: “Manageable is a miserable word.”

Sanya looked back at her.

Mari: “It sounds like someone has put a lid on a fire and wants congratulations because the building hasn’t burned down yet.”

For the first time, Sanya laughed. It was not loud. It slipped out of her as though it had surprised her too.

Sanya: “You’ve had practice with this.”

Mari: “My brother has had the diagnosis since we were young.”

Sanya’s eyes moved to the corridor where Eli had gone. Her expression changed again, but not into pity. Mari had learned to recognize pity quickly. It made people speak more gently and listen less.

Sanya: “I know.”

Mari: “You know him from here?”

Sanya: “Here, and before here. We occupied the same bad rooms at different points in our lives.”

Mari waited.

Sanya tapped two fingers against the sensor patch on her arm. The motion was careful, almost ceremonial.

Sanya: “Arasaka Academy. Applications, interviews, all of it. He got the scholarship. I got a very polite message about how they admired my resilience.”

Mari winced.

Mari: “That is a particularly ugly way to say no.”

Sanya: “They were very expensive words. I assume that was meant to help.”

Mari: “Eli was proud of it.”

Sanya: “Of course he was.”

Mari heard something sharp under the answer and, beneath that, something tired.

Mari: “He worked for it.”

Sanya’s gaze settled on her. Not hostile. Measuring again.

Sanya: “I didn’t say he didn’t.”

Mari should have let it go. Instead, she felt herself smile a little.

Mari: “You were thinking it loudly.”

Sanya: “I have a very expressive silence.”

Mari: “I noticed.”

The patch chirped once more. Sanya looked down at it with open dislike.

Mari: “Does it hurt?”

Sanya’s hand stilled.

Mari almost took the question back. She knew better than to ask strangers about pain as if pain were the whole of them.

Sanya: “Sometimes. Mostly it’s annoying.”

Mari: “That sounds worse.”

Sanya: “It is worse. Pain at least has the decency to announce itself.”

Mari waited. Sanya had the look of someone who had already said more than she meant to, but she did not retreat from it.

Sanya: “Pain is simple, comparatively. It says: there is a problem here. Stop doing that. Take something. Lie down. You can hate it, but at least it has the manners to arrive as itself.”

Her thumb moved over the edge of the patch without quite touching it.

Sanya: “This is different. You put your hand on a rail and your fingers tell you they are closed. Then you look down and they are not. You start checking yourself against the room all the time. Did I pick that up? Is my foot actually where I think it is? Did I feel the heat, or did I only notice because everyone else moved away from it?”

Mari did not interrupt.

Sanya: “After a while, people call it adaptation. Like you have learned a useful trick. Mostly it means you spend half your life translating a body that has stopped speaking clearly.”

The vending machine clicked somewhere behind them. Neither of them looked at it.

Mari: “Eli does that too.”

Sanya’s eyes returned to hers.

Mari: “Not the same way. His hands are worse some days, and he hates when I notice because then I start watching everything else. How long it takes him to stand up. Whether he is leaning on a wall. Whether he is pretending he is not tired.”

Mari had not planned to say any of that. Once it began, it was difficult to make it smaller.

Mari: “I know it makes him angry. I know he is still my brother and not a list of things I should monitor. But sometimes I look at him and I think that if I pay close enough attention, I might catch whatever is coming before it gets him.”

Sanya’s face changed—not soft with pity, but attentive in a way Mari had not expected from someone she had known for less than ten minutes.

Sanya: “Does it work?”

Mari let out a breath that might have been a laugh.

Mari: “No.”

Sanya: “Then you should probably stop.”

Mari looked at her.

Sanya’s mouth moved, almost a smile, but her voice was gentler when she continued.

Sanya: “Or stop telling him every time you notice. Sometimes you want to drop something without watching another person decide what it means about the rest of your life.”

The sentence landed too cleanly. Mari wanted to object to it. Instead, she looked down at the intake screen still open in her hand.

Mari: “You say that like somebody has been watching you.”

Sanya glanced toward the rain-dark windows.

Sanya: “Everybody watches eventually. Parents, doctors, people who mean well. I am not saying they are wrong to be afraid. I am saying fear gets very boring when it is the only thing anyone can see when they look at you.”

Mari folded the intake screen shut.

Mari: “I do not think you are boring.”

Sanya looked back at her. For a second, the sharpness left her face completely.

Sanya: “That is a dangerously generous thing to say to someone you have just rescued from a wire.”

Mari: “I did not rescue you.”

Sanya: “No?”

Mari: “You were going to take half the station with you. That is not the same thing.”

Sanya laughed again, warmer this time.

Eli came back before Mari could ask what Sanya did when she was not standing under bad clinic lights, tethered to a machine that kept calling for her attention.

He walked more slowly than he had gone in, though he was trying to disguise it by checking something on his wrist display. The nurse followed two steps behind him, already halfway turned toward another patient.

Eli: “Good news. They’ve decided I remain medically inconvenient.”

Mari stepped toward him.

Mari: “What did they say?”

Eli looked up. He noticed Sanya beside the check station, then Mari’s face, then the loose lead hanging from Sanya’s wrist. His mouth twitched.

Eli: “They said the same thing they said last time, only with more graphs. We adjust the dosage, I answer more questions next month, and everyone gets to pretend that counts as a plan.”

Mari: “Eli.”

His expression softened by a degree.

Eli: “No emergency. Not today.”

The words should have helped. They did, a little. Mari hated that little.

Sanya adjusted the bag on her shoulder.

Sanya: “Congratulations on remaining inconvenient.”

Eli: “Thank you. I work very hard at it.”

Mari turned to Sanya too quickly, as if she had been caught forgetting something important.

Mari: “Do you have somewhere to be?”

Sanya glanced at the rain behind the clinic windows.

Sanya: “Probably. It will be disappointing if I arrive there.”

Eli gave Mari a look she knew well: go on, then. He never said it when he could make it look like a joke.

Eli: “You should get her number, big sister. Otherwise you’ll spend the rest of the week pretending you only wanted to ask her about the vending machine.”

Mari felt heat rise into her face.

Mari: “I was not—”

Sanya’s smile returned, slower this time.

Sanya: “He’s right. You should.”

Mari opened her contact card and held the screen between them. Sanya touched two fingers to the edge of it. The transfer light blinked once.

Sanya: “There. Now you can warn me if the vending machine finally gives up the can.”

Mari: “That was not what I was going to message you about.”

Sanya: “Good. Surprise me.”

The nurse appeared at the corridor entrance with Eli’s medication packet. Eli took it, and the moment closed around the practical fact of getting him home.

Sanya stepped backward toward the clinic doors.

Sanya: “Try not to arrest him on the way.”

Mari: “No promises.”

Sanya left under the red glow reflected through the rain-dark windows. Mari watched until the doors shut behind her.

Eli: “You watched her leave.”

Mari put the intake screen into his hand.

Mari: “You need a dosage adjustment.”

Eli smiled as if that answered something.
